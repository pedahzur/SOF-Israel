# What We Talk About When We Talk About Special Operations Forces

> Draft for the book's definitional discussion (~1 page). Evidence base: 67 academic and doctrinal definitions, 1944–2018, coded by term and by definitional basis. Working data and coverage map: `~/Documents/Claude/Projects/SOF-Definitions/`. Citations: Chicago 17 notes.

There is no settled definition of special operations forces. NATO doctrine calls them forces "specially designated, organized, trained and equipped … using operational techniques and modes of employment not standard to conventional forces."[^1] A strategist answers that they are "regular troops trained to fight irregularly."[^2] An analyst calls them "a group of intensely committed soldiers" marked by "flexibility, versatility, adaptability."[^3] These are not refinements of one another; they locate the essence in different places—the institution, the mode of fighting, the men. The disagreement is the concept.

It begins with the words. "Special operations forces," "special forces," "commandos," and "elite units" pass as synonyms but carry separate lineages. "Commando" entered English from the Boer wars naming a militia—"a body of burghers called out for military service"—and only in the Second World War hardened into "a unit of Special Service troops … trained for combined operations."[^4] Across NATO armies the same tasks appear under three national titles—Special Operations Forces, Special Forces, Special Designation Forces—the label tracking administrative habit, not difference.[^5]

Within a single term, definitions diverge by what they take to be decisive. Some anchor in designation—a force is special because it falls "under the purview of the US Special Operations Command."[^6] Some in the operators—"the toughest, smartest … most secretive, fittest, best-equipped and consistently lethal killers" in the military.[^7] Some in role and function. The last cuts sharpest: Benjamin Deruelle finds the difference from regular warfare "lay neither in numbers nor in the specialisation of soldiers, but in missions characterised by limited aims, surprise, tricks, stratagems, initiative taken at low levels of command, and constant adaptation"[^8]—setting aside the very operators and organization others treat as constitutive.

These choices track who defines, and when. Scholars writing from outside the institution lean toward role and operator; military and doctrinal authors lean toward designation and organization—the criteria an institution uses to administer itself.[^9] They track the strategic moment too. As the United States built its special operations command in the 1980s and 1990s, definition by designation dominated: the question was which units belonged. After 2001, as the same forces were turned to counterterrorism and irregular war, the literature defined them by function and by operator: the question was what these forces could do.

The definition of a special operations unit is a function of the challenge it was built to meet. Ohad Leslau describes two coexisting approaches to the same forces—a precision "sharpshooter" and a hearts-and-minds "social worker"—that "do not conflict, but rather complement one another."[^10] A category that holds both is defined by its uses, and uses change with place and era. The instability is not a flaw in the literature to be corrected. It is a property of the object: special operations forces are defined into existence, again and again, by the threats their states ask them to answer.
