# What We Talk About When We Talk About Special Operations Forces

*Draft v1 — 2026-06-06. ~820 words. Evidence base: 67 academic and doctrinal definitions, 1944–2018 (`data/clean_v3_master.csv`). Citations: Chicago 17 notes, illustrative.*

---

There is no settled definition of special operations forces. A NATO study can state, with the confidence of doctrine, that they are "military activities conducted by specially designated, organized, trained and equipped forces, using operational techniques and modes of employment not standard to conventional forces."[^1] A strategist can answer, with equal confidence, that they are simply "regular troops trained to fight irregularly."[^2] A defense analyst will say they are "a group of intensely committed soldiers" identifiable by their "flexibility, versatility, adaptability."[^3] These are not refinements of one another. They locate the essence of the thing in different places—in the institution, in the mode of fighting, in the men. The disagreement is not noise around a stable concept. It is the concept.

The instability begins with the words themselves. "Special operations forces," "special forces," "commandos," and "elite units" circulate as if interchangeable, yet each carries its own lineage. "Commando" entered English from the Boer wars, where it named not an elite unit at all but a militia—"a body of burghers called out for military service," closer to the English "militia" than to anything special.[^4] Only in the Second World War did it harden into "a unit of Special Service troops, roughly corresponding in size to a battalion, trained for combined operations."[^5] Colin Gray, writing as a strategist rather than a lexicographer, insists on keeping special operations forces distinct from "elite light infantry raiding forces" and from "special purpose forces"—three categories that the popular vocabulary collapses into one.[^6] Comparison across states compounds the problem. A survey of NATO armies finds the same functional tasks performed under three different national titles—"Special Operations Forces," "Special Forces," "Special Designation Forces"—the label tracking national administrative habit rather than any underlying difference.[^7] Others go further, concluding that such units share "few commonalities internationally other than a shared 'elite' status."[^8]

Even within a single term, definitions diverge according to what they take to be decisive. The corpus sorts cleanly into four bases. Some definitions are anchored in **designation**—a force is special because of where it sits, "under the purview of the US Special Operations Command."[^9] Some are anchored in **organization**—how the unit is selected, trained, and equipped. Some are anchored in the **operators**—"the toughest, smartest, most secretive, fittest, best-equipped and consistently lethal killers" in the military.[^10] And some are anchored in **role and function**—what the force is for. The last category produces the sharpest formulations. Benjamin Deruelle, tracing the sixteenth-century antecedents of the form, finds that the difference between special operations and regular warfare "lay neither in numbers nor in the specialisation of soldiers, but in missions characterised by limited aims, surprise, tricks, stratagems, initiative taken at low levels of command, and constant adaptation."[^11] Here the operators and the organization—the very things other authors treat as constitutive—are explicitly set aside.

These choices are not random. They track who is doing the defining and when. Across the corpus, scholars writing from outside the institution tend to define by role and function or by the qualities of the operator; military and doctrinal authors lean toward designation and organization—toward the criteria an institution uses to administer itself.[^12] The choices also track the strategic moment. Definitions composed in the 1980s and 1990s, as the United States built and defended a unified special operations command, are disproportionately definitions by designation: the urgent question was which units belonged. After 2001, as the same forces were turned to counterterrorism and irregular war, definition by role and function and by the operator rose to the fore: the urgent question was what these forces could do, and who could do it. The basis of the definition shifts with the problem the definer is trying to solve.

This is the point. The definition of a special operations unit is a function of the challenge it was built to meet. Ohad Leslau captures the consequence directly, describing two coexisting approaches to the same forces—one that treats them as a "figurative sharpshooter" carrying out narrow precision tasks, another that casts them in an expansive role, winning "the hearts and minds of the local population"—and concluding that the approaches "do not conflict, but rather complement one another."[^13] A category that can hold both is a category defined by its uses, and its uses change with place and era. The absence of a stable definition is therefore not a failure of the literature to be corrected. It is a property of the object. Special operations forces are defined into existence, again and again, by the threats their states ask them to answer.
