Chapter 3 — The Desert
The British Special Operations Tradition, the Yishuv as Junior Partner, and the Doctrinal Cross-Pollination of the Second World War
Status: First draft of Tranche 1 (§§3.1–3.3), 2026-05-25. Working draft, ~6,800 words. Tranches 2 (§§3.4–3.5) and 3 (§§3.6–3.8) to follow on Ami’s review.  
3.1 The Western Desert, July 1941
Sometime in the first days of July 1941, an officer of the Scots Guards named David Stirling — twenty-five years old, the eldest son of a Scottish baronet, on convalescent leave from a parachute experiment that had gone badly wrong in the desert south of Mersa Matruh three weeks earlier — got out of a hospital bed at the Scottish General Hospital in Alexandria, retrieved his crutches, and made his way by stages to Cairo. He went, by his own subsequent account and by the account that the American journalist Virginia Cowles would publish under his name in 1958, with the specific intention of presenting an operational proposal to the senior officers of the British Middle East Command at their headquarters in Garden City. The proposal was a small one. It concerned a new kind of unit. The unit would be drawn from the dispersed remnants of a recently dissolved Commando formation that had spent the preceding six months attempting, with diminishing success and rising casualties, to conduct amphibious raids against the German and Italian forces along the Libyan coast.[1]
The Layforce of which Stirling had been a member — a brigade of three Commando battalions, named after their commander, Colonel Robert Laycock, that had been sent from Britain to the Middle East in February 1941 — had been dissolved at the beginning of the summer for reasons that Stirling, in the corridors of Middle East HQ on the morning of his crutches walk, was not in a position to dispute. The Royal Navy could no longer spare the destroyers and landing craft on which the Commandos’ amphibious raids depended. The German air pressure over the eastern Mediterranean had become severe. The Crete campaign of late May and early June 1941 had absorbed Layforce in a hopeless defensive battle and had reduced its operational strength by a third in two weeks of rearguard fighting. By the time Stirling was on his feet again, the brigade that had brought him to the Middle East no longer existed.[2]
What Stirling proposed instead, on the morning of his unauthorized visit to Middle East HQ — by his account he was spotted at the main entrance by a guard from whom he had previously fled, abandoned his crutches in order to evade pursuit, and gained the office of the deputy chief of staff, Major-General Neil Ritchie, by an interior route the regular bureaucracy of the headquarters had not been designed to monitor — was that the dispersed Layforce veterans should not be folded back into the conventional infantry units from which they had been drawn six months earlier. They should instead be re-formed, in much smaller numbers and under deliberately irregular command arrangements, as a special unit whose principal operational method would be the airborne and overland insertion of small parties of operators deep behind the German lines for the destruction of aircraft, supply dumps, and communication infrastructure. The cost, Stirling argued, would be small. The potential return — measured in German aircraft destroyed on the ground rather than in the air, in supply convoys disrupted at depth rather than at the front — would be disproportionate to the investment. Ritchie, who had been in the deputy chief of staff’s chair for less than a fortnight and was looking for a reason to push back against the conventional preferences of the staff he had inherited from his predecessor, agreed. The unit Stirling described would be named, by a coincidence of clandestine bureaucracy that the next sections of this chapter will trace, L Detachment, Special Air Service Brigade.[3]
Stirling’s proposal of July 1941 is the founding scene of the British SAS — the unit that, more than any other single Second World War formation, would supply the operational template, the institutional self-understanding, and the cultural genealogy of the post-war national special operations forces that the rest of this book traces. The proposal was also one of two events of that summer, two thousand kilometers apart, that mattered for the larger argument the book is constructing. The other event was unfolding in the Jezreel Valley of Mandatory Palestine, where Yitzhak Sadeh, two months earlier, had begun to assemble the first standing companies of the Palmach — the strike force that, as the previous chapter has described, he had been authorized to establish on the night of the fifteenth of May 1941 in the kibbutz dining hall at Mishmar Ha-Emek. The two events were institutionally unrelated. Stirling did not know that the Palmach existed. Sadeh did not know that Stirling existed. The British Middle East Command in Cairo and the Special Operations Executive’s Cairo station, between them, were aware of both formations and were quietly arranging for the operational collaboration between them that the next four years would deepen and that the Yishuv’s military leadership would, in retrospect, recognize as the formative external influence on the Palmach’s doctrine.[4]
The simultaneity is the chapter’s organizing thesis. In the summer of 1941, under the operational pressure of a German military advance that had run from Norway to the gates of Alexandria in a little under fifteen months, the British Empire and the Jewish national project in Palestine — institutionally separated, politically antagonistic on the question of the post-war future of the Mandate, operationally aligned for the duration of the war against the common Nazi enemy — were independently constructing the same kind of military instrument. The instrument was the small, deliberately unconventional, intellectually heterodox special operations unit. The unit was, on the British side, the SAS that Stirling had just proposed and the Long Range Desert Group that Ralph Bagnold had founded a year earlier and the Special Boat Section that Roger Courtney had pushed into existence in 1940 and the No. 51 Middle East Commando that Henry Cator had recruited from Palestinian Jewish and Arab volunteers in the autumn of 1940. The unit was, on the Yishuv side, the Palmach that Sadeh was building and, within the Palmach, the Mistarvim platoons that would shortly assume the identity-deception operational role that the Brandenburgers, on the German side, had pioneered four years earlier under Theodor von Hippel. The two emerging traditions — the British and the Yishuv — were neither parallel nor identical. They overlapped at specific operational and personnel junctures that this chapter will trace. They diverged in others. They drew, in important measure, on the same European inheritance of irregular warfare doctrine that the previous chapter has identified — the von Lettow-Vorbeck Schutztruppe tradition, the Russian Civil War partisan tradition, the British colonial small-wars tradition that Wingate had carried into the Special Night Squads of 1938. They produced, by the war’s end, two distinct national SOF traditions that would, in the post-war decades, follow institutional trajectories so different that the family resemblance between them would be visible only to historians who took the trouble to look for it.[5]
This chapter takes the trouble. It opens with the German doctrinal challenge that produced the British emergency response. It traces, across the second half of 1940 and the first half of 1941, the improvisational founding of the British SOF ecosystem — the Commandos, the Special Operations Executive, the Special Boat Section, the Long Range Patrol. It moves, with the British command, from London to Cairo, where the Middle East theater became, between the autumn of 1940 and the autumn of 1942, the principal laboratory in which the new units were tested. It returns, in its final third, to the Yishuv — to the small Jewish auxiliary units that the British Middle East Command and the SOE recruited from the Mandate’s Jewish population in those years, to Sadeh’s Palmach and to the operational and doctrinal channels through which the British SOF tradition reached the Yishuv’s emerging strike force. The chapter closes with the post-war dissolutions of October 1945, when the British military establishment, in the institutional reflex that the rest of this book will trace as the recurring fate of national SOF traditions in peacetime, dissolved the units it had built and let the doctrinal inheritance pass into the keeping of the few officers and operators who would, in the next round of wars, reassemble it.
The chronology of the chapter therefore runs from the summer of 1940 through the autumn of 1945 — from Dunkirk to demobilization. It is bracketed by Sadeh’s institutional position on either side: the Palmach he founded in May 1941 will be operating, under his direct supervision, throughout the chapter’s middle section, and will be operating, under Allon’s command after Sadeh’s 1945 hand-off, in the chapter’s closing pages. The Palmach is not, however, the chapter’s principal subject. Its principal subject is the British SOF tradition that the Palmach formed alongside, drew on, and would carry, in its institutional memory, into the IDF that succeeded it. The Palmach is the chapter’s anchor. The British SOF tradition is the chapter’s argument.[6]

3.2 The German Doctrinal Challenge
To understand the speed at which the British military established the operational architecture that Stirling, in July 1941, was attempting to expand, the chapter has to step back, briefly, to the German military innovations of the inter-war period that the British response was constructed against. The German innovations were several, and they were not the principal source of the British SOF tradition that emerged after 1940 — that tradition drew, as the previous section has indicated, on a deeper inheritance of British colonial small-wars practice and on the irregular operational traditions that Wingate and Clarke had carried into the late-Mandate Palestine of the Arab Revolt. But the German innovations supplied the operational problem. The British SOF tradition was, in 1940 and 1941, the empire’s emergency answer to a German military system that, in the eighteen months between the invasion of Poland in September 1939 and the German operations in the Balkans in April 1941, had run rings around every conventional European army that had stood against it.[7]
Three of those German innovations matter for the argument this book is constructing. The first was the Panzergrenadier concept — the mechanized infantry doctrine that the German army had developed in the late 1930s as a complement to the armored formations of the Panzer divisions. The Panzergrenadiers solved the operational problem that the introduction of armor had created for the conventional infantry of the European armies: a foot soldier could not keep up with a tank. Mounted on half-tracks and all-terrain vehicles, the Panzergrenadier infantry advanced with the tanks rather than behind them, cleared obstacles, conducted close-range battlefield reconnaissance, and engaged the anti-tank and anti-aircraft weapons of the defending forces while the armored elements pressed forward against the enemy’s main lines. The combined arms warfare of the German blitzkrieg of 1939–1941 — the operational pattern that overran Poland in five weeks, France in six weeks, and the Balkans in a fortnight — depended on the Panzergrenadier doctrine as much as it depended on the Panzers themselves. The doctrine was, in the eighteen months that followed the fall of France, the canonical example of what the conventional European armies had been overmatched by.[8]
The second German innovation that mattered for the British response was the Fallschirmjäger — the airborne paratroop arm of the Luftwaffe that Hermann Göring had been authorized to establish, on Hitler’s direct instruction, as part of the German pre-war combined arms preparation. The Fallschirmjäger had been an idea before they had been a unit. Soviet airborne demonstrations in the early 1930s, which German liaison officers had observed during the period of cooperation between the Reichswehr and the Red Army, had suggested that paratroops could be inserted behind enemy lines to seize bridges, airfields, and headquarters facilities ahead of the main land advance. The Wehrmacht did not require the Fallschirmjäger in the rapid Polish campaign of September 1939. But on the ninth of April 1940, in the opening hours of Operation Weserübung — the German invasion of Denmark and Norway — Fallschirmjäger paratroops jumped onto and seized two airfields in Aalborg, demonstrating the operational utility of vertical envelopment in a way that no European army had previously witnessed. A month later, on the tenth of May 1940, eighty Fallschirmjäger landed in gliders on the roof of Fort Eben-Emael — the Belgian fortress that had been considered, in the inter-war planning of the French and Belgian general staffs, the strongest fortified position on the European continent — broke through the casemates with hollow charges, and forced its surrender within hours. The Eben-Emael operation opened the German invasion of the Low Countries. The Fallschirmjäger jump on Crete in May 1941 — the operational climax of the Balkan campaign that had absorbed Layforce in its rearguard defensive battle — confirmed, in costlier and more ambiguous terms, that the airborne arm was a permanent feature of the new operational order.[9]
The third German innovation, and the one that mattered most for the operational genealogy this chapter is reconstructing, was the Brandenburger unit of the Abwehr — the German military intelligence service. The Brandenburgers were the German army’s clandestine special operations force. Their founder was Theodor von Hippel, the officer whom the previous chapter has identified as Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck’s other inter-war disciple — the second figure, alongside Yerachmiel Lukacher, who had carried the East African Schutztruppe doctrine into a national military institution that did not yet exist. Where Lukacher had carried von Lettow-Vorbeck’s doctrine into the Gdud Ha-Avoda’s secret military school at Tel Yosef in 1924 and from there, by personnel transmission, into the FOSH and the Palmach of the late 1930s and early 1940s, von Hippel had carried the same doctrinal inheritance into the Bau- und Lehr-Kompanie zur besonderen Verwendung 800 — the Construction and Training Company for Special Employment, Number 800 — that the Abwehr had authorized him to establish on the eve of the war. The unit’s principal innovation was the operational use of identity deception. Brandenburgers spoke the languages of the territories they operated in, wore the uniforms of the local armies or police forces, and were inserted ahead of the main German advance to seize strategic targets — bridges, telegraph stations, headquarters facilities — under the cover of being recognized as friendly local personnel. They had performed, in this role, in Poland in September 1939, in the Low Countries in May 1940, in Yugoslavia in April 1941, and in the opening hours of the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941. The technique was effective and the operational record was substantial. It was also a deliberate violation of the laws of armed conflict — a violation that, on the German side, the Brandenburgers’ commanders had calculated would be answered, by the affected powers, with reprisal and refusal of quarter, and that the Brandenburgers’ operators accepted, when captured, on those terms.[10]
The Brandenburger model was, for the operational genealogy this book is constructing, the German doctrinal answer that the British and Yishuv special operations communities would, in 1941 and 1942, attempt to construct an answer to. The Yishuv’s answer would be the Mistarvim — the platoons of Arabic-speaking Jewish operators, drawn from the Mizrahi and Sephardi Jewish communities of Palestine and from the immigrants from Arab countries who had arrived in the Yishuv in the late 1930s and early 1940s, who would, by 1944, constitute one of the principal arms of the Palmach’s Seventh Company. The British answer would be Captain Herbert Cecil Buck’s Special Interrogation Group — the small unit of German-speaking Jewish operators, drawn from the German Platoon of the Palmach and from the German-Jewish refugees of the Auxiliary Military Pioneer Corps, that Buck would assemble at Marsa Matruh in early 1942 and that would carry out, in May and June 1942, the disastrous raid on the Luftwaffe airfields at Martuba and Derna that the closing section of this chapter will reconstruct. The Brandenburger model would also be answered, on the Soviet side, by the OMSBON brigades of the NKVD that operated behind German lines from 1942 onward, and on the American side, by the OSS Operational Groups that the United States would deploy in occupied Europe from 1943. Five national special operations traditions — German, British, Yishuv, Soviet, American — were, by the closing years of the war, drawing on a single tactical inheritance that the Brandenburgers had pioneered and that von Hippel had carried from his apprenticeship under von Lettow-Vorbeck in inter-war Germany.[11]
The German doctrinal challenge, in other words, was not a single innovation but a system. Panzergrenadier mechanized infantry. Fallschirmjäger airborne envelopment. Brandenburger identity-deception operations. Each of the three innovations corresponded, in the operational architecture of the Wehrmacht and the Luftwaffe and the Abwehr, to a specific operational problem that the conventional European armies of the 1930s had not solved. Each was a deliberate departure from the dominant inter-war doctrinal orthodoxy of trench-and-position warfare. And each was, by the spring of 1941, deployed at operational scale across the European, North African, and Balkan theaters in a combined system that the British military establishment had been unable to defeat by conventional means. What the British emergency response of the summer of 1940 would attempt was, in essence, the construction of an institutional answer to each of the three German innovations, plus a fourth element — the maritime sabotage and coastal raiding capability that the Royal Navy’s diminishing strength in the eastern Mediterranean had created an operational vacuum for. The Commandos, the SOE, the SBS, the Long Range Patrol, the Middle East Commandos, and the SAS, in different combinations and across different theaters, were the British answers to the German system. The Palmach, the Mistarvim platoons, the German Platoon, the SOE Syrian Scheme operators, and the Buck SIG were, on the Yishuv side, the parallel and partly cooperative answers that the Palestine Jewish community produced through its operational collaboration with the British Middle East Command and the SOE Cairo station.[12]
The next section of the chapter takes up the British half of that story. It begins where the chronology of the British emergency response begins — on the evening of the fourth of June 1940, in the closing days of Operation Dynamo, when Winston Churchill, having watched the last of the British Expeditionary Force lifted off the beaches of Dunkirk in the destroyers and trawlers and pleasure boats that the Royal Navy and the civilian flotilla had improvised, rose in the House of Commons and delivered the speech that would, within forty-eight hours, supply the political authorization for the British SOF tradition that the rest of this chapter is about.[13]

3.3 Britain’s Emergency Improvisation, June–December 1940
The chronology of the British SOF tradition begins, by every standard institutional reckoning, on the night of the fourth of June 1940 — when Winston Churchill, three weeks into his prime ministership, addressed the House of Commons on the conclusion of Operation Dynamo. By the morning of the fifth, 338,000 British and French soldiers had been brought home from the beaches of Dunkirk. The British Expeditionary Force had been evacuated; it had not been destroyed; the immediate continental disaster had been averted. But the equipment of the BEF — tanks, artillery, transport, communications — had been left on the French coast. The army that returned to Britain in the first days of June 1940 was a body of trained men without the tools of a modern army to fight with, and it returned to a country that, by Churchill’s calculation, would have to defend itself against a German invasion attempt within sixty days. Operation Sea Lion — the German invasion of Britain — was, in the assessment of the British general staff in early June 1940, the principal operational problem the United Kingdom was about to face.[14]
Churchill’s speech of the fourth of June was the political answer to that assessment. “We shall go on to the end,” the most famous passage of the speech ran. “We shall fight in France, we shall fight on the seas and oceans, we shall fight with growing confidence and growing strength in the air, we shall defend our Island, whatever the cost may be, we shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields and in the streets, we shall fight in the hills; we shall never surrender.” The speech is conventionally remembered as a morale-restoration exercise — the rhetorical apparatus by which the new prime minister rallied a defeated army and a frightened civilian population for the defensive battle that lay ahead. It was also, in the institutional history this chapter is reconstructing, an operational directive. Two of the senior officers at the War Office who heard it — General Sir John Dill, the Chief of the Imperial General Staff, and his military assistant Lieutenant-Colonel Dudley Clarke, whom the previous chapter has introduced as Wingate’s parallel on the British staff in Mandate Palestine of 1937–1938 — took Churchill’s words as the political opening that the operational concept they had been quietly drafting required.[15]
Clarke had been thinking about small-unit raiding operations for at least two months before the Dunkirk evacuation. The thinking had its origins in his own service history. He had observed the operational pattern of the Spanish guerrilla campaign against Napoleon’s army during the Peninsular War of 1807–1814, on which he had read extensively in the inter-war period. He had covered, as a journalist for the Morning Post in 1924, the Berber Rif War in Spanish Morocco, where Abd el-Krim’s irregular forces had pinned down a Spanish colonial army of more than 100,000 men through three years of guerrilla operations in the Atlas Mountains. He had served, in Palestine in 1936–1938, on Wavell’s and Dill’s staffs as a junior intelligence officer during the Arab Revolt and had watched the British Army’s conventional infantry operations against the Arab partisan parties produce, at extraordinary expense in manpower and money, an institutionally embarrassing operational result. The Boer commando model of 1900–1902, on which Clarke’s own reading on the Peninsular War rested, suggested to him that a small, well-led, locally adapted irregular force could produce strategic effects out of proportion to its size — provided the political conditions were created within which such a force could operate without the regular military bureaucracy disowning it.[16]
The political conditions, in Clarke’s reading of Churchill’s evening of the fourth of June, were now in place. On the morning of the fifth, Clarke drafted a one-page memorandum and presented it to Dill in the chief of staff’s office at the War Office. The memorandum proposed the formation of small army units of approximately fifty men each — drawn from existing army formations, trained for amphibious raiding, equipped lightly, and tasked with conducting nuisance operations against the German-held coast of Western Europe. The units would be named, after the Boer model that Clarke explicitly invoked in the memorandum, Commandos. The proposal anticipated the inevitable objections from the conventional infantry establishment by stipulating that the new units would be formed without diversion of resources from the Home Guard. The first commitment Clarke needed, he wrote in the memorandum, was the prime minister’s political authorization. Dill liked the idea. He passed the memorandum to Churchill on the same day.[17]
Churchill, who had read in detail in the inter-war period on irregular warfare and who had, by temperament and by his own brief and ill-fated experience as an officer in the South African War of 1899–1902, an instinctive sympathy for the operational style Clarke was proposing, authorized the Commandos forty-eight hours later. On the sixth of June 1940, in a personal minute to his chief military assistant, General Lionel Hastings Ismay, Churchill set out the political mandate that Clarke would operate under: “Enterprises must be prepared, with specially-trained troops of the hunter class, who can develop a reign of terror down these coasts, first of all on the ‘butcher and bolt’ policy.” The phrase that Churchill chose — “butcher and bolt” — was the British colonial military’s nineteenth-century shorthand for the punitive raiding operations that the regular army had conducted on the North-West Frontier of India against the Pashtun tribal groups of the Khyber Pass. The phrase placed Churchill’s authorization, by deliberate rhetorical reach, in the long British imperial tradition of small-scale punitive operations. The institutional vehicle would be new. The operational style would not be.[18]
With Dill’s backing and Churchill’s authorization in hand, Clarke established, on the seventh of June 1940, a new section within the Military Operations Directorate of the War Office. The section was designated MO9. Its principal task was the formation of the first Commando units. Clarke’s first hire was Major-General Otto Lund, who came in as deputy director of military operations and supplied the bureaucratic cover under which MO9 would operate. Clarke’s other early hires included David Niven — the British actor who had returned from Hollywood at the outbreak of war and had requested combat duty — and a small cadre of younger officers whom Clarke selected on the basis of unconventional intelligence and physical fitness rather than seniority. The first volunteers were drawn from the Army’s Independent Companies, the small ad hoc units that had been raised earlier in 1940 for operations in Norway; from the Royal Marines; and from the regular infantry regiments whose commanding officers were willing, in the summer of 1940, to release men they regarded as marginal to the new units.[19]
The first Commando operation followed within three weeks of MO9’s establishment. Operation Collar, the new units’ inaugural raid, took place on the night of the twenty-fourth to twenty-fifth of June 1940. A small Commando party landed on the French coast in Pas-de-Calais, conducted a brief reconnaissance, killed two German sentries, and withdrew. The operation did not achieve its formal reconnaissance objectives. Its sole British casualty was Clarke himself, who had insisted on joining the raid as an observer and had been wounded by a German rifle round at close range. The operation’s principal effect, from the British command’s perspective, was as cinema. Film crews had been embedded with the raiding party; the footage of small parties of British soldiers in blacked-up faces emerging onto a French beach was released to the cinema newsreels of the United Kingdom in early July; the footage made the Commandos famous in a country whose civilian population was, in those weeks, in the depths of the post-Dunkirk political crisis and was in need of evidence that the war was not being conducted entirely defensively. By the end of July 1940, the number of volunteers for the Commandos exceeded the units’ personnel ceilings by an order of magnitude. By the autumn, the screening process for new entrants had been formalized; selection had become competitive; a training depot had been established in the highlands of Scotland under the direction of Colonel Charles Vaughan; and the operational pattern of the British Commandos as a permanent feature of the British military landscape had been institutionally consolidated.[20]
The Commando initiative was not, however, the only British SOF institution that the summer of 1940 produced. A month after Clarke’s MO9 had been authorized, on the sixteenth of July 1940, Churchill issued the directive that established the Special Operations Executive — the new clandestine warfare agency, formally housed within the Ministry of Economic Warfare under the Labour minister Hugh Dalton, that was charged, in the prime minister’s instruction, with the mandate “to coordinate all action, by way of subversion and sabotage, against the enemy.” The SOE was institutionally distinct from the Commandos. Its operational mandate was different — clandestine sabotage and resistance support, rather than amphibious raiding. Its personnel were drawn from a different recruitment pool — refugee European nationals, multilingual British civilians, intelligence specialists rather than infantry officers. Its institutional culture, by the end of 1940, was already in tension with the parallel intelligence apparatus of the Secret Intelligence Service, whose senior officers regarded the new agency as an institutional rival rather than a complementary tool. But the SOE, like the Commandos, was a product of the same political-strategic moment — the post-Dunkirk improvisation, the willingness of the new Churchill government to fund and to politically protect the unconventional operational instruments that the conventional establishment regarded with skepticism, the recognition that the immediate operational problem facing the United Kingdom could not be solved by conventional means alone.[21]
The third British SOF institution of the summer of 1940 was the Special Boat Section — the small-boat raiding unit that emerged, more by accident than by design, from the persistence of one of the early Commando volunteers. The volunteer was Captain Roger Courtney of the King’s Royal Rifle Corps. Courtney was thirty-seven years old in 1940, an English public-school boy who had spent the inter-war period as a big-game hunter in East Africa and as a sergeant in the British police in Mandate Palestine during the Arab Revolt. He had become, in the course of his African years, an accomplished amateur canoeist; he had once paddled a folding canvas canoe — a folboat — down the length of the Nile. When he volunteered for the Commandos in June 1940, he proposed to the senior staff that the new units should incorporate folboats for close-in reconnaissance and clandestine sabotage of moored shipping. The Royal Navy, in the persons of Admirals Roger Keyes and Theodore Hallett, was skeptical. Courtney did not accept the skepticism. Late one night in July 1940, he paddled a folboat alongside the moored HMS Glengyle, climbed silently aboard, removed the cover of one of the ship’s main armaments, and paddled back to shore without being detected by the Royal Navy watch. Dripping water, he entered the hotel where the senior officers were meeting and presented the gun cover as evidence. A second clandestine boarding, this time conducted under official observation, confirmed the result. By the autumn of 1940, Courtney had been promoted to captain and authorized to raise the No. 1 Special Boat Section — a twelve-man unit, initially attached to the Commandos, that became the institutional ancestor of the post-war British SBS.[22]
The fourth British SOF institution of the summer of 1940 was geographically distant from London and operationally different from the other three. It emerged in the Western Desert, in the headquarters of the Commander-in-Chief Middle East at Cairo, where Major Ralph Bagnold — the Royal Signals officer whose inter-war expertise in desert reconnaissance had been built up across two decades of private exploration of the Libyan Desert — approached General Sir Archibald Wavell in the first days of July 1940 with a proposal. The proposal concerned the geographic problem the Middle East Command was about to confront. With Italy’s declaration of war on the tenth of June 1940 and Mussolini’s deployment of his colonial army into Libya for an advance on the Suez Canal, the British defensive position in Egypt depended on the small Western Desert Force’s ability to monitor and to disrupt Italian operations across an area of half a million square miles of largely impassable terrain. Bagnold proposed that the Middle East Command authorize a small, motorized reconnaissance and raiding unit that would penetrate the Italian rear areas by overland desert routes, using vehicles that Bagnold himself had modified across the inter-war period for sand-dune operation, and that would report back on Italian movements while conducting low-level harassment of supply convoys and outposts. Wavell, who had been familiar with Bagnold’s work since their parallel service in Palestine in 1937–1938, approved the proposal on the third of July 1940. The unit was established under the designation No. 1 Long Range Patrol Unit. Bagnold’s friends in the Middle East would later refer to it, more accurately, as the Long Range Desert Group.[23]
By the end of the summer of 1940 — three months after Dunkirk — the British military had stood up the Commandos, the SOE, the SBS, and the LRP. Four new institutional vehicles, each authorized at the political level, each operating outside the conventional command structures of the army and the navy, each tasked with a different operational problem, each in active development. None of the four had yet produced operational effects on a scale that the conventional military establishment regarded as significant. All four were, by the standards of the established service institutions, under-resourced, institutionally insecure, and politically vulnerable. But all four existed. They had been authorized. They had been staffed. They had been allowed to begin to develop the operational doctrine and the personnel ecology that, by the middle of 1942, would make them the most institutionally productive component of the British Empire’s military response to the war.[24]
The institutional centrifuge that the summer of 1940 had set in motion in London would, by the end of the year, move east. In November 1940, the British army established the Special Service Brigade — the institutional umbrella under which the expanding Commando units were grouped into five battalions. The same month, the No. 51 Middle East Commando was raised at the British base at Geneifa, near the Bitter Lake in the Canal Zone, under the command of Lieutenant-Colonel Henry Cator. The new commando was the first of three British units to be drawn substantially from the Jewish and Arab volunteers of the Auxiliary Military Pioneer Corps — the British army labor formation that, since the outbreak of war, had absorbed the Mandate’s foreign-national volunteers, including the Yishuv’s Jewish manpower that the Mandate authorities considered suitable for labor service but politically problematic for combat employment. The 51 Middle East Commando’s 360 volunteers, drawn primarily from the Yishuv, would constitute one of the principal Yishuv–British operational interfaces of the early war, and would, after the unit’s disbandment in 1941, supply the personnel from which Buck’s Special Interrogation Group would later be assembled. On the eighteenth of December 1940, Wavell formally requested that Clarke be transferred from London to Cairo to take charge of the Middle East Command’s strategic deception apparatus. Clarke arrived in Cairo on the same day. His official designation was as the regional director of MI9, the British escape-and-evasion organization; his actual remit was the establishment of what would, four months later, become the dedicated military deception organization known as ‘A’ Force. Clarke would remain in Cairo for the remainder of the war.[25]
By the end of 1940, the British SOF ecosystem had migrated, in personnel and institutional weight, from the offices of MO9 at the War Office in London to the Middle East Command’s headquarters in Cairo. The reasons for the migration were operational: the principal theater of British ground operations against the Axis was Wavell’s Western Desert Force; the principal opportunity for the kind of small-unit raiding and reconnaissance that the new institutions were designed for was in the Libyan Desert; the principal command authority willing to protect the unconventional units against the conventional staff was Wavell himself, whose Palestine staff in 1937–1938 had included both Wingate and Clarke and who had carried, into the Middle East Command, an institutional appetite for irregular operations that the conventional army establishment did not share. Cairo, in the first months of 1941, became the laboratory in which the British SOF tradition would be elaborated and in which the Yishuv’s parallel operational instruments would, by accident and by deliberate British policy, be drawn into operational collaboration with the British units. The next section of this chapter takes up that story.[26]

3.6 The Yishuv as Junior Partner, June 1941 – June 1942

On the morning of the eighth of June 1941, a twenty-six-year-old Haganah officer named Moshe Dayan led a small reconnaissance party from a forming-up position in the Galilee panhandle, across the international frontier into Vichy-controlled Lebanon, and toward a French police post at a site that British military maps recorded as Khirbet Iskandaroun, a few kilometers north of the Litani River. The party numbered, by the standard reconstructions, thirty-one men — twenty-one Australians of the 7th Division's 2/16th Battalion, eight Palmach operators drawn from the Yishuv volunteers who had been trained over the preceding three months by the Special Operations Executive's Syrian Scheme team at Mishmar Ha-Yam on the Carmel coast, and two officers of the Special Operations Executive's Cairo station who were operating in the field as liaison and operational supervision. Dayan was the senior officer of the Palmach element. The party's mission was a forward seizure of the police post and the road bridges to the north of it, ahead of the Australian 7th Division's main advance across the same terrain, which would begin at dawn on the same day as the opening phase of Operation Exporter — the Allied invasion of Vichy Syria and Lebanon that the British, Australian, Indian, and Free French commands had been planning, in coordination with the SOE Cairo station and the Haganah liaison apparatus, since the early weeks of 1941. The bridge was seized within the first hour. The police post was taken with light French resistance. Approximately ten in the morning, while Dayan was on the roof of the captured police post observing the next French defensive line through a pair of British military binoculars, a Vichy French rifle round struck the binoculars at a near-perpendicular angle. The lenses shattered. Glass and metal fragments drove inward into Dayan's left eye socket. The eye was destroyed. Dayan was evacuated to a field hospital on the British side of the frontier within four hours and to the hospital at Haifa within twenty-four. He would, for the remainder of his life, wear the black eye patch that would, in the decades after the founding of the Israeli state, become one of the most recognized visual signatures of any military and political figure in the modern history of the country.[48]

The Iskandaroun action of the eighth of June 1941 was, in the operational record of the British–Yishuv collaboration of the war's middle years, one of the early and most consequential field demonstrations that the Haganah's operators could be inserted into the SOE Cairo station's clandestine apparatus as functional equals rather than as auxiliary labor. The institutional architecture that had made the action possible had been assembled in stages across the preceding eighteen months. In the autumn of 1940, the SOE's Cairo station, operating under cover of the Inter-Services Liaison Department designation, had opened a quiet channel of operational coordination with the Haganah's intelligence apparatus in Jerusalem, mediated by David Hacohen — the Haganah's senior contact within the Solel Boneh construction company, whose Mandate-licensed engineering contracts had supplied a cover under which Haganah personnel and matériel could be moved across the eastern Mediterranean — and Reuven Zaslani, the Haganah Intelligence Service officer who would, after the founding of the Israeli state seven years later, change his surname to Shiloah and become the institutional architect of the Mossad. In the early months of 1941, with the Vichy French position in Syria and Lebanon becoming a strategic problem for the British Middle East Command's planning, the SOE Cairo station had been authorized to expand the Mishmar Ha-Yam training facility into a formal SOE field station and to begin training Yishuv operators for clandestine insertion into Vichy territory. Maurice Oldfield — the same Oldfield who would, in the post-war decades, become the chief of the Secret Intelligence Service — was the station's principal officer on the SOE side. Tony Simonds was the principal British training officer. The Yishuv side of the arrangement, conducted on Sadeh's authority after his appointment as Palmach commander in May 1941 and with the explicit approval of the Haganah's command apparatus in Jerusalem, was managed in the field by Yigal Allon and by Yitzhak Sadeh's principal training officer for irregular operations, Yitzhak Hochman.[49]

The Yishuv operators trained at Mishmar Ha-Yam in the spring of 1941 — Dayan and his Iskandaroun party were the first cohort to be inserted operationally — were organized within the SOE's clandestine apparatus as the German Platoon (Plugat HaGermanim) and the Mistarvim (HaMistarvim; literally "those who become like Arabs"). The two platoons differed in their operational mission. The German Platoon was the platoon of German-speaking operators, drawn principally from the German-Jewish immigrants of the late 1930s who had reached Palestine on illegal-immigration ships from Trieste and Marseille between 1937 and 1939, and was trained for clandestine insertion into German and Italian rear areas under German uniform and German linguistic cover. The Mistarvim was the platoon of Arabic-speaking operators, drawn principally from the Mizrahi and Sephardi Jewish communities of Palestine — Yemenite, Iraqi, Syrian, and Maghrebi Jews who had grown up in Arabic-language households and who could pass, in the appropriate dress and with the appropriate linguistic cover, as Palestinian Arabs or as Lebanese, Syrian, or Iraqi Arabs — and was trained for clandestine insertion into Arab-majority areas of the Levant in indigenous cover. Both platoons drew, doctrinally, on the operational template that the Brandenburger establishment had pioneered under Theodor von Hippel four years earlier in Germany. Both platoons drew, institutionally, on the British SOE infrastructure — the training facility, the SOE-supplied uniforms and matériel, the SOE-coordinated insertion and extraction. Both platoons would, by the spring of 1942, be operating in the field in coordination with the corresponding British units. The German Platoon supplied the principal operator pool for Captain Herbert Cecil Buck's Special Interrogation Group, which the SOE Cairo station authorized in January 1942. The Mistarvim supplied the principal operator pool for the SOE Syrian Scheme's continuing clandestine operations in Vichy Syria, in Lebanon after the September 1941 Allied occupation, and in the Iraqi and Persian operations that the SOE conducted in support of the British suppression of the pro-Axis Rashid Ali coup in Baghdad in May 1941 and the subsequent British–Soviet occupation of Iran in August.[50]

The most operationally consequential collaboration between the Yishuv's clandestine units and the British SOF establishment of 1942 was the Special Interrogation Group. Buck had assembled the SIG at Mersa Matruh in February 1942, on the authority of the SOE Cairo station and with the operational support of the SAS that David Stirling had, by that date, established at Kabrit. The unit's initial strength was approximately forty operators. Roughly thirty were German-Jewish refugees drawn from the Auxiliary Military Pioneer Corps and from the German Platoon's Mishmar Ha-Yam cohort. The remainder were British officers and specialist personnel who supplied the operational planning and the field coordination with the SAS and the LRDG. Training at Mersa Matruh through February and March 1942 was conducted to a level of operational realism that exceeded, in its insistence on linguistic and cultural cover, anything the British SOE establishment had previously attempted. Operators were required to speak German exclusively for periods of weeks at a time. They were required to maintain German military identities — name, rank, unit, hometown, regimental detail — under interrogation pressure that simulated, as closely as British training methods of the period could simulate, the conditions of German rear-area arrest. They wore German uniforms — the Afrika Korps tropical pattern, sourced from the captured stocks that the British had seized in O'Connor's December 1940 offensive and that the SOE Cairo station had reserved for clandestine use. They carried German small arms, German rations, German personal effects. They were inserted, in operational deployments, in German military vehicles — captured Volkswagen Kübelwagen utility cars and Mercedes-Benz staff cars, repainted in Afrika Korps markings — which the SIG operators had been trained to drive and to service in the German manner. The unit's first operational deployments, in March and April 1942, were small-scale reconnaissance and intelligence-collection operations against German rear-area positions in eastern Cyrenaica. The operations were successful. The operational technique appeared to be viable.[51]

❓ שאלה לעמי: הסיג (SIG) — אני מתאר את האימון בעוצמה הזו (40 לוחמים, 30 יהודים-גרמנים, אימון על Tobruk כקטע אופרציוני וכו'). יש לי גם פה את האפשרות לעבור ישר לאסון של מרטוּבּא/דרנא (יוני 1942), או להשהות עוד פסקה אחת על המורכבות הזהותית והפסיכולוגית של היהודים-גרמנים שלבשו מדים גרמניים והעבירו את ימיהם בעיברית-גרמנית חצויה. השאלה: האם הספר רוצה להיכנס לעומק הפסיכולוגי כאן, או להישאר במישור האופרציונלי-מוסדי?

The Martuba and Derna airfield raid of the night of the thirteenth to fourteenth of June 1942 was the SIG's first major operational deployment in collaboration with the SAS, and was, in the operational record of the British–Yishuv SOF collaboration of the war's middle years, the unit's institutional catastrophe. The operation had been planned by Stirling and Buck in coordination, with SOE Cairo station authorization, as a coordinated assault on the four principal Luftwaffe airfields in the Martuba and Derna area — installations from which the German close air support assets for Rommel's June 1942 advance on Tobruk were being staged. The operational concept depended on the SIG's identity-deception cover. A SIG party — German-uniformed, German-speaking, mounted in repainted German vehicles — would, at the planned hour of the operation, drive openly through the perimeter security of the airfield's outer cordon, present authentic-looking German movement orders to the perimeter guards, and reach the airfield's principal aircraft dispersal areas before the German garrison had time to identify them as hostile. Once inside the perimeter, the SIG operators would silence the immediate German security with suppressed weapons. A larger SAS raiding party, following the SIG penetration on foot from a lying-up point outside the perimeter, would then enter the airfield's interior and place Lewes bombs on the parked aircraft in the manner established in the earlier SAS airfield raids. The planning was sound. The operational rehearsals were thorough. The SIG cover party that drove to the Martuba perimeter on the night of the thirteenth was led by a SIG operator whose German cover name was Brueckner and whose actual identity — the subject of considerable post-war reconstruction — was that of a German-Jewish refugee who had reached Palestine in 1939, had volunteered for the AMPC in 1940, had been recruited into the German Platoon's Mishmar Ha-Yam cohort in 1941, and had been transferred to Buck's SIG in February 1942.[52]

What occurred at the Martuba perimeter shortly before midnight on the thirteenth of June 1942 has been reconstructed, in the post-war literature, from the testimony of the few SIG and SAS operators who survived the operation and from the German operational records of the Luftwaffe security units that detected and responded to the British penetration. The reconstructions diverge on the question of agency. The standard British version, drawing on the post-war debriefing of the surviving SAS officer Lieutenant David Russell, holds that the SIG operator Brueckner, immediately on arriving at the Martuba perimeter, broke from the cover party, walked openly toward the German guard post, and informed the German sentry — in fluent German, without coercion — of the operation's composition, the planned penetration point, and the location of the SAS lying-up area. The German garrison, alerted to the raid before the SAS party had reached the perimeter, mounted an ambush at the lying-up area as the SAS party was forming up for the perimeter assault. The ambush killed approximately fourteen SAS operators outright. Six SIG operators who had been with the cover party at the perimeter were captured and were, within a small number of days, executed under Hitler's Kommandobefehl — the Commando Order of the eighteenth of October 1942 that would, in its formal promulgation four months after the Martuba operation, codify the German military's practice of executing all Allied special operations personnel on capture without regard for the conventional protections of the laws of armed conflict, but that had already been informally in effect against SIG operators since the Brandenburger establishment's identification of the SIG as an operational mirror-image of their own identity-deception units. Two SAS officers — Captain Augustin Jordan and Lieutenant Russell — escaped the ambush. They reached the British lines, on foot, across one hundred and forty miles of German-controlled desert, eight days after the operation. The SIG cover party did not survive. Buck himself, who had not been with the field party on the night of the operation, withdrew the remaining SIG personnel to the SOE Cairo station compound within seventy-two hours of the operational confirmation of the failure. The SIG was institutionally suspended within a week. It was formally disestablished by the SOE Cairo station in October 1942, four months after the Martuba operation, and its surviving operators were dispersed back to the AMPC, the Palestine Regiment, the Palmach, or — in a small number of cases — to other SOE clandestine operations in the Mediterranean theater.[53]

The Martuba disaster ended the SIG. It did not end the operational collaboration between the British SOF establishment and the Yishuv's clandestine units. Through the second half of 1942 and into 1943, the SOE Syrian Scheme's continuing operations in the Levant — directed against the residual Vichy French intelligence apparatus in Lebanon and Syria after the September 1941 Allied occupation, against the Italian and German covert agent networks that the SIME had identified as operating in Syria and Iraq, and against the Axis-aligned Iraqi political elements that had survived the British suppression of the Rashid Ali coup of May 1941 — continued to draw on the Mistarvim operator pool. The German Platoon's surviving operators were absorbed, in the institutional reorganization of the Palmach that Sadeh authorized in the autumn of 1942, into the Palmach's emerging Seventh Company — the company-sized formation that would, by 1944, consolidate the Palmach's specialized intelligence, reconnaissance, and clandestine-operations cadres into a single institutional vehicle and would, in the operational and personnel history that Chapter 4 will reconstruct, supply the principal Yishuv-period precedent for the IDF's post-1948 special-reconnaissance establishment. The institutional architecture of the British–Yishuv SOF collaboration, in other words, survived the Martuba disaster. The specific operational vehicle — Buck's SIG — did not. The operational pattern the SIG had pioneered — German-speaking Jewish operators in German uniform conducting identity-deception operations against German rear-area positions — would not be reattempted, at unit scale, in the remaining years of the war.[54]

The wider institutional consequence of the SIG's brief operational career, for the Yishuv side of the wartime collaboration, was the institutional confirmation that the British SOE establishment was willing to absorb the Yishuv's existing clandestine apparatus on terms of effective operational equality. The Mishmar Ha-Yam training facility was, by the autumn of 1942, the central institutional node through which the Haganah's intelligence and clandestine-operations cadres were being trained to the operational standard the British SOF establishment had developed across 1940 and 1941. The German Platoon and the Mistarvim were, by the same date, institutionally absorbed within the SOE's regional operations apparatus on terms that gave the Haganah operational and personnel control over the deployment of its own people. The institutional precedent the collaboration established — that the Yishuv's clandestine apparatus could be operated within a larger Allied SOF establishment as a junior but functionally equal partner, with operational control retained at the Yishuv level — would survive the war, would survive the post-war dissolution of the British SOF establishments that this chapter is about to take up, and would supply the institutional template under which, in the founding war of the Israeli state of 1948, the same Mistarvim platoons and the same German Platoon veterans would be deployed against the Arab and the British forces in operations that the previous generation's British trainers had not anticipated.[55]

3.7 The Closing Wartime Arc, October 1942 – August 1945

The institutional turning point of the entire British wartime SOF establishment, as of the Yishuv's wartime relationship with the British apparatus, was the Second Battle of El Alamein. The battle opened on the night of the twenty-third of October 1942, in the operational sector approximately sixty miles west of Alexandria where Field Marshal Bernard Montgomery — appointed to the Eighth Army command in August 1942 in the institutional shake-up that had also brought General Sir Harold Alexander to the Cairo theater command — had concentrated, across the preceding nine weeks, an Allied force of approximately 195,000 men, 1,029 tanks, 908 artillery pieces, and 530 operational aircraft. The German–Italian Panzer Army Africa under Rommel, depleted by the Allied air interdiction of Axis logistics across the Mediterranean through the summer and by Rommel's own decision to commit his last strategic reserves in the failed Battle of Alam Halfa in early September, fielded approximately 116,000 men, 547 tanks, 552 artillery pieces, and 350 operational aircraft against Montgomery's deliberately constructed defensive operation. The battle lasted thirteen days. Montgomery's combination of conventional armored breakthrough, infantry consolidation, and persistent air pressure broke the Panzer Army Africa's operational coherence on the third of November and produced, on the fourth, Rommel's first major operational retreat of the war. By the eleventh of November 1942, the Eighth Army had pursued the Axis remnants three hundred miles west to Mersa Matruh. By the twenty-third of November, the British had reentered Tobruk, retaken in five weeks the ground it had taken Rommel eighteen months to conquer. By the early weeks of January 1943, the Eighth Army was approaching the Tunisian frontier, in operational coordination with the United States First Army's Torch landings in Algeria and Morocco of November 1942. The North African campaign would conclude with the surrender of Army Group Africa at Tunis on the thirteenth of May 1943. The strategic position of the British Middle East Command, and of the SOF establishment the command had built around itself, was transformed.[56]

The transformation, for the SOF establishment, was double-edged. The institutional space that the conventional staff had conceded to the unconventional units during the operational crisis of 1940 and 1941 was, by the spring of 1943, beginning to contract. The SAS, expanded under Mayne's command into two regiments and operating from the spring of 1943 in the Italian theater rather than the Western Desert, was now operating in a strategic environment in which the operational rationale for small-unit airfield raiding was, for the first time since the unit's establishment, less compelling than the conventional alternative of Allied air superiority. The LRDG, whose deep-desert operational specialism had been the indispensable institutional asset of the British operations in 1941 and 1942, was operationally unemployed in the Mediterranean theater after the Tunisian campaign's conclusion. The SBS, which had operated successfully alongside the Greek and Italian resistance through 1943 and 1944, was institutionally over-extended by the end of the war. The Commando establishment, which had been used at scale in the Normandy landings of June 1944 and in the subsequent campaign in northern France and the Low Countries, had completed by the spring of 1945 the operational function the conventional army staff regarded as the unit type's principal institutional justification. By the early months of 1945, the conventional army establishment was beginning, in the institutional language of the period, to "regularize" the SOF formations — to absorb them back into the conventional order of battle, to subject them to the standard establishment table, to limit their operational autonomy, and, in the cases the conventional staff regarded as the most institutionally aberrant, to dissolve them outright.[57]

The Yishuv's institutional position, across the same closing arc of the war, ran in the opposite direction. The Palmach, which Sadeh had constituted in May 1941 with three companies of approximately three hundred operators, had grown by the middle of 1944 to approximately one thousand operators distributed across six companies, two of which — the Sixth and the Seventh — were specialized formations rather than conventional infantry. The growth had been achieved through an institutional arrangement that Sadeh and his successor as Palmach commander, Yigal Allon — who had taken command on the fifteenth of October 1945, six months after the European armistice and one month before the end of the chapter's wartime arc — had negotiated with the kibbutz movement. Under the arrangement, Palmach operators would, after their initial training, be permanently stationed at member kibbutzim of the Kibbutz Movement's three principal federations. Each operator would spend approximately fourteen days per month on operational training and operational deployment, and approximately sixteen days per month working as agricultural labor in the host kibbutz's fields, dairy operations, and construction projects. The arrangement solved, in a single institutional move, two operational problems the Palmach had faced from its founding. It solved the funding problem — the Palmach had no independent income, and the British Mandate authorities had withdrawn the operational subsidy they had extended through 1941 and 1942 once the immediate Axis threat to the Mandate had receded after El Alamein. It solved the personnel problem — by tying Palmach service to host kibbutzim, the arrangement created a permanent operator pool whose members could be mobilized rapidly without the institutional friction of conventional army recruitment and dispersal. It produced, as an institutional side-effect, a Palmach culture that would, in the post-war decades, become indistinguishable in important respects from the kibbutz movement's broader political and ideological culture. The institutional pattern would have profound consequences for the post-1948 IDF's relationship with the Palmach's institutional inheritance, consequences the next chapter will take up at length.[58]

The Yishuv's broader contribution to the British wartime SOF establishment, in the closing two years of the war, expanded through two channels the operational rationale this chapter has not yet traced. The first channel was the Yishuv Parachutist Mission to occupied Europe, conducted jointly by the SOE Cairo station and the Haganah's intelligence apparatus between February 1943 and the European armistice of May 1945. The mission inserted, by parachute and by clandestine ground infiltration from neutral and partisan-controlled territory, approximately thirty-seven Palestinian Jewish operators into Yugoslavia, Hungary, Slovakia, Bulgaria, Romania, and Italy. The operators' mission was double — they were to coordinate with the local Allied intelligence and resistance apparatus in the territories of their insertion, and they were to make contact, where contact was possible, with the surviving Jewish communities of the occupied territories to facilitate clandestine rescue and emigration to the Yishuv. The operational record was mixed. Twelve of the thirty-seven operators were captured. Seven were executed by the German or the collaborationist authorities of their territories of operation, including the parachutist operator Hannah Szenes, who was captured shortly after her insertion into Hungary in June 1944 and was executed in Budapest in November 1944, and the parachutist operator Enzo Sereni, who was inserted into northern Italy in May 1944, was captured within forty-eight hours, was transferred to Dachau concentration camp in late 1944, and was executed there in November 1944. The institutional importance of the mission, in the operational and doctrinal arc this chapter is reconstructing, was not the operational success of the insertions — the operational record was, in narrow military terms, modest — but the institutional confirmation that the Yishuv's clandestine apparatus could conduct strategic-depth insertions into European territory in coordination with the SOE and the relevant national resistance movements. The institutional template would, in the post-war decades, become an essential element of the Israeli intelligence apparatus's strategic operational doctrine.[59]

Authorized by the British War Cabinet on the twentieth of September 1944, after five years of Yishuv lobbying that had been resisted by the British Mandate authorities and by the Foreign Office on the political grounds that an organized Jewish military formation would, after the war, prefigure the founding of a Jewish state that the Mandatory power did not wish to authorize, the Brigade was formally constituted in November 1944 from the three Palestine Regiment battalions that had been operating in the Mandate and the Middle East theater since 1942. The third Yishuv institutional development of the closing wartime arc was the formal separation, on the seventeenth of April 1945, of the Palmach Naval Company — the Palyam — from the Palmach's conventional infantry organization. The Palyam had originated, in 1943, as a small naval-operations cadre within the Palmach's Sixth Company. It had been operating, under SOE coordination, against the Italian and German shipping in the eastern Mediterranean and along the Lebanese and Syrian coasts. After the April 1945 separation, the Palyam was reorganized as an independent Palmach sub-unit under the command of Yochai Bin-Nun, with a strength of approximately one hundred operators trained in maritime sabotage, underwater demolition, small-boat reconnaissance, and the coastal infiltration operations that the unit would, in the post-war period of British blockade of illegal immigration to Palestine, conduct against the Royal Navy's blockade vessels in the Mediterranean. The Palyam's institutional inheritance — the maritime SOF tradition that the unit would carry into the post-1948 IDF naval establishment — would supply the doctrinal and personnel foundation of the Israeli naval special operations apparatus that the post-war decades would build into a strategic capability of national consequence.[61]

The Yishuv had become, in institutional terms, a small national military establishment that operated at the operational standards of a major Allied power — for the immediate reason that the wartime collaboration with the British SOF establishment had taught it to operate at those standards, and for the longer reason that the institutional architecture the Haganah and the Palmach had developed across the war years had been engineered to absorb and to internalize the operational doctrine the British collaborators had transmitted.[62]

3.8 The Dissolutions of October 1945

The institutional reflex that the British Army staff carried into the post-war period was, by the late summer of 1945, already in motion against the SOF establishment that the wartime emergency had created. The first formal dissolution came on the first of October 1945, when the War Office issued the order that disbanded both regiments of the Special Air Service. The order had been drafted, in the War Office's establishments branch, on the standard institutional grounds that the SOF formations had been raised for the specific operational requirements of the wartime emergency and that the conclusion of the war removed the operational rationale for the formations' continuing existence. Mayne, by then a lieutenant-colonel and the senior surviving SAS officer of the wartime period, accepted the dissolution without formal protest. The institutional space within which Mayne might have contested the dissolution had been closed off by the broader political pressure on the British army establishment to demobilize at scale. The SAS, an institutionally aberrant formation that had been politically protected through the war by Churchill and operationally protected by Wavell and Alexander, had no comparable patron in the post-war Labour government that had taken power in July 1945 on the platform of post-war demobilization and the institutional reconstruction of the welfare state. Mayne returned to his pre-war legal practice in Newtownards, Northern Ireland. He would, across the next decade, conduct one more substantial operational deployment — the Falklands Islands Dependencies Survey expedition of 1945–1946, in which he served as the survey's commander of land operations on the Antarctic Peninsula — and would die in a road traffic accident outside Newtownards in December 1955, at the age of forty.[63]

The dissolutions cascaded. The Long Range Desert Group was disbanded on the first of August 1945, two months earlier than the SAS, in an institutional sequence the War Office's establishments branch did not separately justify. The Special Boat Service — the wartime SBS, which had operated through the Mediterranean and the Aegean as a small permanent unit of approximately one hundred operators since 1942 — was disbanded in October 1945. The Commando establishment was dissolved in November 1945, with the exception of the three Royal Marines Commandos, which were retained in the Royal Marines' permanent establishment and would, after the institutional reconstruction of 1946, supply the post-war British amphibious special operations capability that the Korean War and the Suez Crisis would call back into action. The Special Operations Executive was the institutional last to be dissolved. SOE was formally wound up by an order of the fifteenth of January 1946, with its files transferred to the Foreign Office and the Secret Intelligence Service, its operational personnel demobilized or absorbed into the SIS or the SIS's clandestine successor organizations, and its institutional memory dispersed across a series of post-war debriefing volumes and official histories that would, in the case of the official history that William Mackenzie completed in 1948, be held under classification for fifty-five years before being released in 2000.[64]

The institutional inheritance the dissolutions did and did not transmit divided, in the post-war reckoning, along three lines. The first line was the institutional persistence within the British army of a small number of officers who had served in the wartime SOF formations and who would, in the operational and political opening of the late 1940s, push for the reconstitution of the units. David Stirling himself was central. Stirling had been released from German captivity in May 1945 at the end of the European war. He had returned to Britain, had been demobilized, and had moved, in 1946, to Southern Rhodesia, where he had attempted across the late 1940s to build a private commercial empire that would underwrite his subsequent political activities in Africa and the Middle East. From his Rhodesian base, Stirling had remained in contact with the dispersed network of wartime SAS officers — Brian Franks, John Woodhouse, the brothers Stirling collectively — who would, in the operational opening that the Malayan Emergency of 1948 would create, push the War Office to reconstitute a Special Air Service formation for the operational requirements of the colonial counter-insurgency campaign in the Malayan jungle. The reconstitution was authorized in 1950. The Malayan Scouts (SAS) of 1950, redesignated 22 SAS Regiment in 1952 and consolidated within the regular British army establishment in 1959, would supply the institutional continuity through which the wartime SAS doctrinal package would survive the post-war dissolutions and would be carried into the operational opening of the Cold War and the post-colonial conflicts of the 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s. The institutional path was indirect and was operated by a small group of officers whose personal commitment to the wartime template was the principal institutional resource that the reconstitution depended on.[65]

The second line of inheritance was the institutional persistence within the Yishuv of the Palmach as a standing strike force, in which the wartime operational collaboration with the British SOF establishment had produced a doctrinal package that the Palmach's institutional architecture had been engineered to absorb. The Palmach itself would, in the institutional convulsion of November 1948, be dissolved by Ben-Gurion in the same War of Independence-period decision that integrated the Haganah's military formations into the institutional structure of the new Israel Defense Forces. The dissolution would scatter the Palmach's personnel across the conventional infantry, armored, paratrooper, and naval establishments of the new IDF, and would actively suppress, in its institutional execution, the distinct operational doctrine that the Palmach's wartime training and operational experience had developed. The doctrinal package the Palmach had absorbed from the British SOF establishment of 1940 to 1945 — the SAS template that the SIG operational collaboration had transmitted, the SOE clandestine operational technique that Mishmar Ha-Yam had institutionalized, the LRDG deep-reconnaissance pattern that Bagnold's wartime operations had supplied, the Brandenburger identity-deception model that Mistarvim and German Platoon had operated against — would pass, in the post-1948 dispersal, into the private operational memory of the dispersed Palmach veterans. The third line of inheritance was the doctrinal one that operated independently of either of the institutional vehicles. The British SOF tradition of 1940 to 1945 had produced, in the operational record of its principal units and in the published memoirs and operational histories of its principal commanders, a body of doctrinal material that would, across the following four decades, become canonical reading for the founders and the commanders of the post-war national SOF establishments of the major and minor powers. The Cowles biography of Stirling, published in 1958 and rapidly translated into the principal European languages and into Hebrew, supplied the founding scene that the SAS-derived units of the post-war period would invoke in their own institutional founding documents. The Foot official history of SOE, although held under classification until 1999, circulated within the British intelligence and special operations community across the late 1940s and the 1950s in officer-only versions that influenced the doctrinal development of the post-war British clandestine apparatus. The Lloyd-Owen memoir of the LRDG, published in 1980, would supply the founding institutional memory for the deep-reconnaissance and long-range patrol doctrine that the post-war American, Australian, and New Zealand SOF establishments would build their own deep-reconnaissance units around. The German operational analyses of the Brandenburger establishment, available in fragmented form in the post-war German military literature and in the captured German documents that the United States and British intelligence services had inventoried in 1945 and 1946, would supply the doctrinal substrate for the identity-deception operational pattern that the Yishuv's Mistarvim of the late 1940s, the IDF's reconstituted Mistarvim of the 1950s and 1960s, and the post-1980 Israeli special-reconnaissance establishment would adapt for the operational requirements of the Israeli–Arab conflict. The doctrinal inheritance, in other words, operated as the institutional substrate of the post-war national SOF traditions even where the wartime institutional vehicles had been formally dissolved.[67]

The chapter closes, then, with an institutional irony. The British SOF establishment of 1940 to 1945 had been built, on the operational evidence of its origin, on the proposition that the conventional military bureaucracies of the major powers could not, by themselves, produce the unconventional operational instruments that the German doctrinal challenge of the late 1930s and early 1940s had created the operational requirement for. The establishment had been built, between June 1940 and July 1942, by a small number of unconventional officers operating in institutional positions of political protection — Churchill in London, Wavell in Cairo, Dill at the War Office, Alexander at Cairo after Wavell, Ritchie at the Eighth Army's deputy chief of staff's office in the moment of Stirling's July 1941 visit — and against the institutional preferences of the conventional army staff. The establishment had produced, across five years of operational deployment in the principal theaters of the war, the institutional and the doctrinal inheritance that the post-war national SOF traditions would build on. And then, with the war's conclusion, the same conventional army staff that had been institutionally reluctant about the SOF formations across the war had dissolved them, at administrative speed, in the autumn of 1945. The institutional vehicles that the wartime emergency had produced had not survived the wartime emergency. The doctrinal inheritance had survived, in the personal operational memory of the dispersed officers and operators who had served in the wartime formations and who would, in the next round of wars, supply the institutional resources for the reassembly of the operational tradition. The Yishuv's institutional position, on either side of the chapter's bracketing dates, had been the unusual position of a small national community whose own military establishment had been engineered, by deliberate institutional design across the period of the British collaboration, to absorb the doctrinal inheritance the wartime collaboration was transmitting and to preserve it through the post-war institutional dissolutions on the British side. The next chapter takes up the period — from the November 1947 United Nations partition vote through the 1948 founding war, the IDF's institutional consolidation of 1949 to 1953, and the operational and institutional opening that the 1956 Sinai Campaign would produce in 1957 — in which the doctrinal inheritance the present chapter has reconstructed would, on the Yishuv-and-then-Israeli side, be reassembled into the operational instrument that the rest of the book is about.[68]
